1. An Apology and a Lesson

Yesterday we published a paper claiming remarkable results for a Golden Ratio-based memory system. That paper was wrong. We had data leakage—our model was seeing future tokens (T_next) during training, which is cheating in causal language modeling.

This is the risk of Open Notebook Research: we move fast, we publish quickly, and sometimes we get it wrong. The alternative—waiting months for perfect results—means slower progress for everyone. We choose transparency over perfection, which means we must also be transparent about our failures.

The corrected results tell a different but ultimately more interesting story.

---

2. What This Actually Is

Before we called this a "memory system." That was wrong.

What we actually built is a learnable exponential moving average (EMA) layer—a low-pass temporal filter that provides momentum, not retrieval. This places our work in the lineage of:

- Classical EMA filters from signal processing
- S4 and other structured state space models
- Hyena and similar convolutional approaches

We are not claiming novelty in the mechanism. The contribution is showing that a simple phi-decay filter, interleaved with attention, improves both quality and speed.

---

3. Why the Golden Ratio?

We use phi = 0.618 as our decay constant. The name "Golden Ratio Engram" is historical rather than principled—we started with phi for aesthetic reasons and kept it when it worked.

We have not found evidence that the golden ratio is special. Other decay constants in the 0.5-0.8 range perform similarly. The decay constant is a hyperparameter.

What matters is the effective timescale. For phi = 0.618:

    half-life = ln(0.5) / ln(phi) = 1.44 tokens
    phi^10 = 0.006 (99.4% decayed after 10 tokens)
    phi^20 = 0.00004 (essentially zero)

This is a very local smoother. It helps with immediate next-token coherence, not long-range dependencies. The "engram" framing was misleading—this is closer to a 2-3 token moving average than any kind of memory.

4. Three Types of Memory in Transformers

Before presenting our results, we need to understand what "memory" means in a transformer. There are three distinct types:

4.1 Memory in Weights (Parametric Memory)

When a transformer is trained, it stores knowledge in its billions of parameters. When GPT-4 knows that "Paris is the capital of France," that fact is encoded somewhere in its weight matrices—compressed, distributed, and implicit.

- Capacity: Enormous (billions of parameters)
- Access: Slow (requires forward pass through layers)
- Updatable: Only through training
- Nature: Crystallized knowledge, not episode-specific

4.2 Memory in Activations (Working Memory / Context)

The KV-cache in attention is working memory. It holds the specific tokens from the current conversation. When you ask a follow-up question, the model "remembers" your previous messages because they're still in the activation space.

- Capacity: Limited by context window
- Access: Fast (direct attention lookup)
- Updatable: Grows with each token
- Nature: Episode-specific, transient
- Mechanism: Content-addressable retrieval via dot product

4.3 Temporal Momentum (What Engrams Provide)

This is what we built. Not memory—momentum. A directional bias accumulated over time. It doesn't store "what was said" but rather "where we've been heading."

- Capacity: Fixed (single state vector)
- Access: Instant (no lookup needed)
- Updatable: Continuous decay and accumulation
- Nature: Low-pass filter, not retrieval
- Mechanism: Exponentially weighted moving average

The key insight: Engrams provide direction, not content. You cannot query an engram for specific information. It's like asking "what's the wind direction?" vs "what did I say five sentences ago?"

Mathematically:

Attention = content-addressable memory (lookup by similarity)
Engram = low-pass temporal filter (smooth by recency)

5. Why Engrams Can't Replace Attention

The Mechanics of Attention Lookup

Query:  q = "What is the capital of France?"
Keys:   k1 = "The weather is nice"
        k2 = "Paris is a city"
        k3 = "France has a capital"

Scores: q dot k1 = 0.1  (low relevance)
        q dot k2 = 0.6  (moderate relevance)
        q dot k3 = 0.9  (high relevance)

Output: Weighted sum, dominated by k3's value

The dot product measures alignment between vectors. This is content-addressable lookup: you find information by describing what you want, not by knowing where it is.

Why Engrams Can't Do This

An engram accumulates a running sum with decay:

h_t = phi * h_{t-1} + x_t

After 100 tokens, h_100 contains traces of all previous tokens, but they're superimposed, not indexed. The engram state is rank-1 with respect to time—it collapses history into one direction. There's no way to ask "what was token 47?"

This means:
- No retrieval
- No disambiguation between past events
- Catastrophic interference when multiple distinct facts matter

Analogy: Attention is like a library with a card catalog. Engrams are like a river—everything that fell in is downstream somewhere, but you can't fish out a specific item.

This is why pure engrams fail catastrophically (PPL 3000+). If they had worked, we would distrust our own results.

6. What Engrams Actually Provide

If engrams can't retrieve, why do they help?

6.1 Temporal Smoothing (Momentum)

The engram tells the model "where the conversation has been trending." It's not "Alice said X," it's "we've been discussing topics in the Alice-direction." This is useful for local coherence and next-token prediction.

6.2 Recency Weighting

The phi-decay (0.618 per step) naturally emphasizes recent tokens. After ~20 tokens, old information has decayed to <1% influence. This provides a soft "attention to recent past" without the O(N^2) cost.

6.3 Cheap Global Context

Computing an engram is O(N) with O(1) memory. It gives every position a summary of "everything before" without storing everything before.

In signal processing terms: the EMA acts as a low-pass filter, amplifying low-frequency trends and suppressing high-frequency noise in token embeddings. The transfer function is:

    H(z) = 1 / (1 - phi * z^-1)

This is the simplest possible infinite impulse response (IIR) filter.

7. The Architecture That Works

After correcting our data leakage, we found:

7.1 Engram Alone: Fails Completely

Model                       Val PPL     Notes
Pure Engram (8 layers)      3,000+      Severe overfitting
Gated Engram                3,600+      Worse
Magnitude-Gated             3,700+      Even worse

This is exactly what theory predicts. A rank-1 temporal collapse cannot support language modeling.

Lesson: Engrams cannot replace attention. They provide momentum but not retrieval.

7.2 Engram + Attention: Works

Model                       Val PPL     vs Baseline
Pure Attention (8 layers)   8.19        baseline
Interleaved 4A + 4E         7.92        -3.3%

Interleaving engram layers with attention layers gives a modest quality boost. The attention provides retrieval; the engram provides temporal smoothing.

This is similar in spirit to:
- ALiBi (recency bias in attention)
- Convolutional layers in hybrid architectures
- SSM layers in Mamba-style models

We are not claiming a novel mechanism—just a simple, effective configuration.

7.3 The Speed Problem

But the original engram had a Python loop:

for t in range(seq_len):
    state = phi * state + gate(x[t]) * proj(x[t])

This loop killed performance: 15,774 TPS vs 230,992 TPS for pure attention.

8. The Vectorized Solution

8.1 The Mathematical Insight

The recurrence h_t = phi * h_{t-1} + x_t has a closed form:

h_t = sum from i=0 to t of phi^(t-i) * x_i

This is a weighted sum where older tokens get exponentially less weight. We can compute this via:

1. Weight each input by phi^(-i)
2. Cumulative sum
3. Weight each output by phi^t

powers = phi ** torch.arange(seq_len)
inv_powers = 1.0 / powers

x_weighted = x * inv_powers.view(1, -1, 1)
cum_sum = torch.cumsum(x_weighted, dim=1)
output = cum_sum * powers.view(1, -1, 1)

This is the same cumsum trick used in S4, Hyena, and other modern SSMs to make recurrences GPU-friendly. We are not claiming novelty here.

8.2 The Gate Simplification

The original engram had a state-dependent gate: gate = sigmoid(x dot h_{t-1}). This creates a true sequential dependency and is harder to optimize.

We simplified to an input-dependent gate: gate = sigmoid(W dot x). This can be computed in parallel for all positions.

Classic deep learning lesson: simpler + parallel often beats theoretically expressive but sequential.

8.3 The Full Fast Engram Layer

class FastEngramLayer(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim):
        self.proj = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.gate_proj = nn.Linear(dim, dim)
        self.phi = 0.618  # 1/phi, the golden ratio

    def forward(self, x):
        B, L, D = x.shape

        # Parallel gate and projection
        gate = torch.sigmoid(self.gate_proj(x))
        x_gated = gate * self.proj(x)

        # Vectorized phi-decay via cumsum
        powers = self.phi ** torch.arange(L, device=x.device)
        x_weighted = x_gated * (1.0 / powers).view(1, -1, 1)
        cum_sum = torch.cumsum(x_weighted, dim=1)
        output = cum_sum * powers.view(1, -1, 1)

        return output

9. Results

9.1 Quality: Modest Improvement

Model                   Val PPL     Change
Pure Attention          8.19        baseline
Sequential Engram       7.92        -3.3%
Fast Engram             7.86        -4.0%

The ~4% improvement is real but not revolutionary. This is a useful incremental gain, not a paradigm shift.

The simplified input-dependent gate works slightly better than the state-dependent gate—likely due to easier optimization.

9.2 Speed: Context-Dependent

Model                       TPS         Change
Pure Attention              230,992     baseline
Sequential Engram           15,774      -93%
Fast Engram                 259,307     +12%
Fast Engram + compile       341,396     +48%

Important caveats on speed:

- These measurements are at 512 token context (WikiText-2)
- Engram layers are O(N), attention is O(N^2)
- The relative speedup grows with sequence length
- Some gains come from torch.compile optimizations and kernel fusion
- Your mileage will vary based on hardware and batch size

At short contexts, attention is already cheap. The speed advantage becomes more pronounced at longer sequences.

10. Why This Matters

10.1 For Practitioners

You can add a phi-decay layer to any transformer as a lightweight temporal bias:
- Modest perplexity improvement
- Potential speed improvement at longer contexts
- Minimal code changes

10.2 For Researchers

This demonstrates that:
1. Linear recurrences can complement attention without replacing it
2. The cumsum trick makes EMA-like operations GPU-friendly
3. Input-dependent gates can match state-dependent gates while being easier to optimize

10.3 For the Field

The obsession with "replacing attention" may be misguided. Augmenting attention with cheap temporal features is a more practical path. Attention handles retrieval; temporal filters handle smoothing. They solve different problems.

11. Limitations and Future Work

What We Don't Know

1. Scaling: These results are on a 30M parameter model with WikiText-2. Behavior at GPT-3 scale is unknown.

2. Task Sensitivity: Engrams help language modeling perplexity. Do they help reasoning? Code? Math? Unknown.

3. Optimal Decay: We used phi without systematic comparison to other decay constants.

4. Layer Placement: We used alternating A-E-A-E. Other patterns may work better.

Promising Directions

1. Multi-scale decay: Instead of one phi, use a bank of 4-8 different decay rates:

   h^(k)_t = phi_k * h^(k)_{t-1} + x_t

   This is a diagonal state-space model (SSM)—essentially the simplest possible S4/Hyena layer. Our single-decay engram is the degenerate case with one eigenvalue. Stacking multiple timescales would give multi-resolution temporal context and likely outperform our current design.

2. Long context testing (2k-8k+ tokens): This is where O(N) vs O(N^2) really matters.

3. Attention/engram ratio sweep: Try 6A+2E, 4A+4E, 2A+6E to find the quality-speed frontier.

4. Scale to 1B+ parameters and test on diverse benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, etc.)

---

12. Conclusion

We started with a broken experiment (data leakage), discovered why pure engrams fail (rank-1 temporal collapse, no retrieval), and ultimately found that engrams work as a lightweight temporal bias when combined with attention.

The final system:
- Interleaved attention + fast phi-engram layers
- ~4% perplexity improvement (modest but real)
- Speed improvement at longer contexts
- Simple vectorized implementation using standard cumsum tricks

This is not a new memory system. It's a learnable EMA filter that complements attention. The mechanism is well-understood from signal processing and SSM literature. The contribution is empirical: this particular configuration works, and here's the code.

Open notebook research means publishing mistakes alongside successes. Yesterday's paper was wrong. Today's results are correct—until proven otherwise.
